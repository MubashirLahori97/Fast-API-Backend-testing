# Lesson 5: The Hypothalamus's Role in Thyroid and Adrenal Disorders

## The hypothalamus regulates the adrenals and thyroid [Established]

- **Adrenals:** stress response, blood pressure, blood glucose, and fat and protein use
- **Thyroid:** cellular metabolism, mitochondrial energy output, glucose and fat metabolism

The hypothalamus must function well for the adrenals and thyroid to function well. It directs both through the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis.

### Three routes to the adrenal glands [Established]

- The **sympathetic nervous system** stimulates the adrenal medulla to release catecholamines.
- The **hypothalamus**, through CRH and pituitary ACTH, controls the adrenal cortex's release of glucocorticoids.
- The **renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system** stimulates the adrenal cortex's outer layer to release mineralocorticoids.

## Central disorders of the thyroid and adrenals

Hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction underlies four central disorders: central hypothyroidism, central hyperthyroidism, central adrenal insufficiency and Cushing's disease. [Established] In Deborah's experience, milder hypothalamic dysfunction is often at the root of thyroid and adrenal symptoms that standard testing misses. [Clinical observation]

### Central hypothyroidism

**Lab pattern** [Established]: low free T4 (and often free T3) with a TSH that is low, normal, or even mildly elevated. A "normal" TSH does not exclude central hypothyroidism, because the TSH produced may be biologically less active.

**Hypothalamic dysfunction in patients with persistent "thyroid symptoms"** [Emerging; preliminary data]

A 2014 study presented at the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists (Sakkal et al.) found that obese patients with ongoing thyroid symptoms despite normal thyroid levels may have hypothalamic dysfunction. These patients were often labeled as malingering, as having borderline personality disorder or "adrenal depletion," or as having uncharacterized disorders, "when hypothalamic dysfunction would explain most."

The study classified hypothalamic dysfunction as likely with 3 of the following symptoms and definite with more than 3. Prevalence of each symptom in the study population:

| Symptom | Prevalence |
| --- | --- |
| Weight change | 88% |
| Mood disorders | 80% |
| Fatigue | 76% |
| Pain | 72% |
| Changes in sleep | 70% |
| Temperature dysregulation | 68% |
| Sympathetic or parasympathetic complaints | 64% |
| Libido issues | 38% |

68% met criteria for definite and 22% for likely hypothalamic dysfunction. Trigger point tenderness, found in 68%, was an important physical finding.

This was a conference abstract, not a peer-reviewed paper; treat it as preliminary.

### Central hyperthyroidism [Established]

- Elevated free thyroid hormones with an unsuppressed (normal or high) TSH
- Usually a TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma (0.5 to 2% of pituitary adenomas)
- HPT function depends on local hypothalamic neurotransmitters and gut-brain hormones

### Central adrenal insufficiency [Established]

- **Primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease):** adrenal gland failure; low cortisol with high ACTH.
- **Central (secondary or tertiary) adrenal insufficiency:** hypothalamic-pituitary failure; low cortisol with low or inappropriately normal ACTH.
- Central AI is more common than primary AI, with an estimated prevalence of 150 to 280 per million. The most common cause overall is suppression of the HPA axis by glucocorticoid medications.

### Cushing's syndrome [Established]

- About 80% of endogenous hypercortisolism is ACTH-dependent. Most of that comes from ACTH-secreting pituitary adenomas (Cushing's disease); the rest from ectopic ACTH.
- Hypercortisolism driven by excess hypothalamic CRH is very rare.
- Functional hypercortisolism (pseudo-Cushing's state), seen with chronic stress, depression, alcohol use, obesity and poorly controlled diabetes, reflects HPA overactivity and resolves when the cause is treated. This is where hypothalamic dysfunction is most relevant. [Emerging]

## Hormone chemistry [Established]

| Class | Examples | Properties |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peptide hormones | CRH, TRH, ACTH, TSH | Chains of amino acids; water-soluble; act on cell-surface receptors |
| Amine hormones (derived from amino acids) | Catecholamines; thyroid hormones (T4, T3) | Catecholamines are water-soluble. Thyroid hormones are lipophilic, mostly protein-bound, and act on nuclear receptors, like steroids. |
| Steroid hormones | Glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids, adrenal androgens | Made from cholesterol; insoluble in blood, so mostly protein-bound |

- Normally only about 5 to 10% of glucocorticoids circulate free.
- Only about 0.02% of T4 and 0.3% of T3 circulate free.

## Thyroid hormone

### Thyroid hormone effects [Established]

- **Heart:** thyroid hormones have a permissive effect on catecholamines, increasing beta-receptor expression to raise heart rate, stroke volume, cardiac output and contractility.
- **Lungs:** stimulate the respiratory centers, increasing oxygenation.
- **Skeletal muscle:** increase development of fast-twitch (type II) fibers.
- **Metabolism:** raise basal metabolic rate; induce fat breakdown or synthesis; stimulate carbohydrate metabolism and protein building; catabolic at high levels.
- **Children:** act with growth hormone to stimulate bone growth; support brain maturation through axon growth and myelination.

### Thyroid testing

| Test | Typical reference range |
| --- | --- |
| TSH | 0.4 to 4.0 mIU/L |
| Free T4 | 0.8 to 2.3 ng/dL |
| Free T3 | 2.0 to 7.0 pmol/L |
| Reverse T3 | 9.2 to 24.1 ng/dL |
| Anti-TPO antibodies | <30 IU/mL |
| Anti-thyroglobulin antibodies | <116 IU/mL |
| Thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulin (TSI) | <1.3 index (130%) |

Ranges vary by laboratory. Free T4 and free T3 have largely replaced total hormone measurement because they perform better diagnostically. [Established]

Supporting tests in hypothyroidism:

- CBC: anemia
- Lipid profile: hyperlipidemia
- Metabolic panel: reduced GFR, raised creatinine, dilutional hyponatremia
- Creatine kinase: may be elevated
- Prolactin: may be elevated

## Adrenal hormones [Established]

**Cortisol**

- Acts on many nuclear and cell-surface receptors; mediates fast cell signaling
- Maintains blood pressure by sensitizing blood vessels to vasoconstrictors and suppressing vasodilators such as nitric oxide
- Suppresses the immune system
- Increases gluconeogenesis and reduces glucose uptake in tissues
- Activates fat breakdown and stimulates fat cell growth
- In excess, causes muscle wasting, bone loss and skin thinning

**DHEA**

- Converts to anabolic androgens
- Drives adrenarche, which precedes gonadarche in puberty
- Main source of circulating testosterone in women

**Aldosterone**

- Increases sodium reabsorption and potassium excretion in the kidney
- Water follows sodium, increasing circulating volume and blood pressure

### Diurnal cortisol dysregulation [Emerging]

- Cortisol normally peaks shortly after waking and falls to its lowest at night.
- The World Health Organization recognizes that prolonged low-level psychological stress can over-activate neuroendocrine, cardiovascular and immune systems.
- A dysregulated HPA axis produces high nighttime cortisol, raising the risk of cardiovascular disease, stroke, diabetes, hypertension, metabolic syndrome and mental illness.

### Adrenal testing

| Test | Typical reference range |
| --- | --- |
| Morning (8 am) cortisol | 5 to 25 mcg/dL |
| ACTH | 10 to 60 pg/mL |
| DHEA-S and unconjugated DHEA | Age- and sex-specific |
| Pregnenolone | 22 to 327 ng/dL |
| Aldosterone | 7 to 30 ng/dL |

Also check prolactin, HbA1c and a lipid profile. Circulating DHEA-S is the best measure of adrenal androgen excess. [Established]

## Prevalence of adrenal disorders [Established]

- Addison's disease: 100 to 140 per million
- Central adrenal insufficiency: 150 to 280 per million; more common in women and between ages 30 and 50
- Cushing's syndrome: rare, more common in women and between ages 25 and 50

## Adrenal insufficiency

### Symptoms and signs

| Symptoms | Signs |
| --- | --- |
| Fatigue: "wired and tired," afternoon slump | Weight change |
| Progressive weakness, muscle aches | Hyperpigmentation (primary AI), vitiligo |
| GI symptoms: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea | Central fat with sarcopenia |
| Salt cravings | Orthostatic hypotension |
| Heightened sense of smell, taste and hearing | |

**History:** other autoimmune conditions, prior steroid use, brain radiation or surgery.

**Orthostatic blood pressure:** orthostatic hypotension is a drop of at least 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within 3 minutes of standing. [Established] Deborah also watches for a systolic pressure that fails to rise by about 10 mmHg on standing from lying, as a sign of low aldosterone. [Clinical observation]

### Diagnosis [Established]

1. Check morning cortisol.
    - Below 3 to 5 mcg/dL: low. Check ACTH. High ACTH means primary AI; low or normal ACTH means central AI.
    - 5 to 15 mcg/dL: indeterminate. Do a cosyntropin stimulation test.
    - Above 15 mcg/dL: AI ruled out.
2. **Cosyntropin stimulation test:** measure cortisol at baseline and at 30 and 60 minutes after 250 mcg cosyntropin IV or IM. A peak of 18 mcg/dL or more is normal; below 18 suggests AI. Newer, more specific cortisol assays use lower cutoffs (about 14 to 15 mcg/dL).

### Treatment

**Hypothalamic support** [Product] [Clinical observation] — nutraceutical support to regulate the HPA axis, support healthy adrenal function, and support adrenal steroid metabolism and receptor function.

**Conventional treatment** [Rx overview]

- **Primary AI:** glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement, usually for life. DHEA may help some women.
- **Central AI:** glucocorticoid replacement in 2 or 3 divided doses, typically 15 to 20 mg hydrocortisone daily. Deborah adds DHEA and adaptogens. [Clinical observation]

**Diurnal cortisol dysregulation** [Clinical observation] — adaptogens; homeopathic adrenal cortex.

## Hypercortisolism (Cushing's)

### Signs and symptoms [Established]

- Progressive proximal muscle weakness
- Hypogonadism: in women, menstrual irregularities, amenorrhea, infertility, low libido; in men, low libido and erectile dysfunction
- Depression, cognitive problems, emotional lability
- Hypertension, peripheral edema, left ventricular hypertrophy, impaired diastolic function
- Peptic ulcer
- Galactorrhea, hypothyroidism
- Avascular necrosis of the hip
- Visual field defects (pituitary adenoma)

### Testing [Established]

- **24-hour urinary free cortisol:** above the assay's upper reference limit (historically above 100 mcg/24 h).
- **Overnight 1 mg dexamethasone suppression test:** 1 mg by mouth at 11 pm to midnight; 8 am cortisol below 1.8 mcg/dL indicates normal suppression and makes Cushing's unlikely.
- **Dexamethasone-CRH test:** cortisol above 1.4 mcg/dL suggests Cushing's.
- **ACTH:** low or undetectable means an adrenal source (ACTH-independent). Normal or high means a pituitary or ectopic source (ACTH-dependent).
- **High-dose dexamethasone suppression test:** helps distinguish pituitary Cushing's disease from ectopic ACTH. Ectopic sources include pancreatic islet cells, lung, thyroid and thymus tumors.

### Treatment [Rx overview]

- **First line:** surgical removal of the pituitary tumor.
- **Second line:** medication to normalize cortisol: steroidogenesis inhibitors, somatostatin analogs to suppress ACTH, or glucocorticoid receptor antagonists.
- Treat comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension.

- **Hydrocortisone** (can be compounded), dosed to mimic the daily rhythm: 10 to 20 mg in the morning and 5 to 10 mg at 3 to 5 pm

Patients with true adrenal insufficiency must not interrupt glucocorticoid replacement and need stress dosing for illness, injury or surgery. The one-day-off approach applies only to supporting diurnal dysregulation, not to confirmed AI. [Established]

### Adaptogens [Emerging]

Phytoadaptogens act partly through the stress-sensor protein Hsp70, which plays a role in cell survival.

- **Ashwagandha** (*Withania somnifera*), standardized to 5% withanolides, 300 mg daily: significantly lowered serum cortisol in stressed healthy adults after 56 to 60 days.
- **Panax ginseng**, standardized to 7% ginsenosides, 100 to 300 mg daily: regulates the HPA axis.
- **Siberian ginseng** (*Eleutherococcus senticosus*), 200 to 600 mg daily: may lower cortisol.
- **Rhodiola rosea**, 340 to 680 mg daily: helps modulate the cortisol awakening response.
- **Schisandra chinensis**, 500 mg daily: supports HPA function.

### Glandulars and homeopathics [Clinical observation; limited published evidence]

- **Adrenal glandulars:** whole adrenal gland extracts may support adrenal function. Limit use to 3 to 6 months. Historical use of thyroid, pancreatic, adrenal, thymus and liver extracts suggests glandulars can be beneficial, but controlled evidence is limited.
- **Homeopathic adrenal cortex:** used by Deborah to help reset the daily cortisol rhythm. One dropperful under the tongue at 8 am and half a dropperful at 3 to 5 pm; not after dusk.

## The thyroid-adrenal connection [Established]

Thyroid and adrenal function are interdependent. Thyroid hormone affects HPA function, cortisol affects TSH secretion and T4 to T3 conversion, and both are directed from the hypothalamus.

## Thyroid disorders

### Prevalence [Established]

- About 200 million people worldwide have thyroid disease; 40% of the world's population is at risk of iodine deficiency.
- More than 12% of the US population will develop thyroid disease in their lifetime.
- Hypothyroidism: about 4.6% of the US population, up to 20% of older adults; subclinical hypothyroidism in up to 10% of adults.
- Hyperthyroidism: about 1.2%.
- Hashimoto's thyroiditis is the most common cause of hypothyroidism in the US after age 6. Graves' disease affects about 0.5%. Both are more common in women.

### Causes

**Hypothyroidism** [Established]

- Hashimoto's thyroiditis
- Radiation, radioactive iodine
- Hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction
- Hormonal transitions: pregnancy, menopause
- Drugs: amiodarone, lithium, interferon, interleukin-2, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, thalidomide, stavudine, bexarotene, perchlorate, rifampin, ethionamide, phenobarbital, phenytoin, carbamazepine

**Hyperthyroidism** [Established]

- Graves' disease
- Overactive thyroid nodules
- Thyroiditis
- Excess iodine. In iodine-deficient nodular goiter, iodine intake can trigger thyrotoxicosis, because iodine acts as both an immune stimulant and a substrate for hormone synthesis.
- Excess thyroid hormone medication
- TSH-secreting pituitary tumor
- Complications include pericardial thickening and bone loss.

### Autoimmune thyroiditis [Established]

| Hashimoto's | Graves' |
| --- | --- |
| Autoimmune, with lymphocyte infiltration and fibrosis | Autoimmune; TSI stimulates the TSH receptor continuously |
| Female sex; middle age | Female sex; pregnancy |
| Infections; iodine; drugs (lithium, amiodarone, interferons, interleukins, antiretrovirals) | Mental stress; smoking and alcohol |
| Genetics (HLA); other autoimmune disease | Genetics and family history |
| Excess iodine; radiation | Pregnancy |

### Hypothyroidism: symptoms [Established]

- Fatigue, lethargy; falling asleep when sitting
- Weight gain with decreased appetite
- Constipation
- Menstrual disturbances, impaired fertility
- Cold intolerance, reduced sweating
- Paresthesias, nerve entrapment syndromes
- Dry skin, hair loss
- Blurred vision, decreased hearing
- Throat fullness, hoarseness
- Muscle and joint pain, limb weakness
- Sleep apnea
- Depression, emotional lability, slowed speech and movement
- Forgetfulness, poor memory and concentration

### Hypothyroidism: signs [Established]

- Dry skin, pallor, carotenemia (yellowish skin)
- Coarse, brittle hair; thick, brittle, ridged nails
- Loss of scalp, axillary and pubic hair, and the outer third of the eyebrows
- Dull expression, coarse features, enlarged tongue, puffy eyes
- Goiter, hoarseness
- Galactorrhea
- Lower systolic and higher diastolic pressure
- Bradycardia, pericardial effusion
- Abdominal distention; ascites (uncommon)
- Hypothermia (severe cases only)
- Myxedema (non-pitting edema); pitting edema of the legs
- Delayed reflex relaxation, ataxia

### Lab patterns in hypothyroidism [Established]

| TSH | Free T4 | Free T3 | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| High | Low | Low | Primary hypothyroidism |
| High (>10 mIU/L) | Normal | Normal | Subclinical hypothyroidism, high risk of progression |
| High (6 to 10 mIU/L) | Normal | Normal | Subclinical hypothyroidism, low risk of progression |
| High | High | Low | Impaired T4 to T3 conversion (congenital, or amiodarone) |
| High | High | High | Peripheral thyroid hormone resistance |
| Low, normal or mildly high | Low | Low | Central hypothyroidism, or recent withdrawal of excess thyroid hormone |
| Inappropriately normal | Normal or low | Low (high reverse T3) | Nonthyroidal illness syndrome |

Hashimoto's: anti-TPO and anti-thyroglobulin antibodies; 10 to 15% of cases are antibody-negative.

### Hypothyroidism: treatment

- **Hypothalamic support** [Product] [Clinical observation]: to address common central causes and support thyroid hormone receptor function and healthy immune balance.
- **Thyroid hormone replacement** [Rx overview]: synthetic T4; desiccated thyroid (T4/T3); compounded T4/T3.

## Hyperthyroidism

### Symptoms and signs [Established]

| Symptoms | Signs |
| --- | --- |
| Nervousness, anxiety, hyperactivity | Tachycardia or atrial arrhythmia |
| Increased sweating, heat intolerance | Systolic hypertension with wide pulse pressure |
| Palpitations | Warm, moist, smooth skin |
| Muscle weakness | Lid lag, stare |
| Increased appetite with weight loss | Hand tremor |
| Infrequent periods | |

### Diagnosis [Established]

- Suppressed TSH with elevated thyroid hormones.
- **Subclinical hyperthyroidism:** low but detectable TSH with normal free T4 and T3.
- **Nonthyroidal illness** can temporarily suppress TSH; repeat tests before treating subclinical disease.
- **Graves':** elevated TSI; anti-TPO antibodies elevated in about 85%.

### Treatment [Rx overview]

- Radioactive iodine
- **Methimazole:** inhibits thyroid hormone synthesis by blocking iodine oxidation; does not block peripheral T4 to T3 conversion. Available as 5 and 10 mg tablets; well absorbed; half-life 6 to 8 hours. Propylthiouracil is preferred in the first trimester of pregnancy and in thyroid storm; avoid methimazole with allergy or intolerance.
- Optimizing the HPA axis: Sacred Seven amino acids only. [Product] [Clinical observation]

## Thyroid hormone replacement [Rx overview]

### Is T3 therapy necessary? [Established]

- Tracer studies show that serum T3 reflects tissue T3 content and signaling in most tissues, but not in the brain and pituitary.
- This holds for healthy people and for hypo- and hyperthyroidism, but not for nonthyroidal illness syndrome.
- Consider adding T3 when free T3 stays low despite adequate free T4 and appropriate TSH and the patient remains symptomatic, especially with mood symptoms. [Emerging]
- A T4/T3 combination may be a starting point for severely symptomatic patients. [Clinical observation]
- Too much T3 causes palpitations, nervousness and insomnia.

### Dosing overview [Established]

**Levothyroxine (T4)**

- Starting dose about 1.6 mcg/kg/day; lower (about 1 mcg/kg/day, or 25 to 50 mcg) in older adults and those with heart disease
- Titrate by 12.5 to 25 mcg every 4 to 6 weeks
- Average adult dose 100 to 125 mcg daily; children may need up to 4 mcg/kg/day
- Full effect on TSH takes about 6 to 8 weeks
- Take in the morning on an empty stomach
- In central hypothyroidism, TSH cannot guide dosing; aim for free T4 (and free T3) in the mid to upper normal range

**Liothyronine (T3)** — rapidly absorbed with a short half-life; 20 to 60 mcg daily in 2 to 3 divided doses.

**Combination** [Clinical observation] — about 20 to 30% T3 and 70 to 80% T4 to normalize TSH and ease symptoms; desiccated thyroid is one option.

### Wilson's protocol: context [Clinical observation]

The Wilson's protocol uses cyclic sustained-release T3 based on low body temperature and symptoms, not lab values, aiming to deplete T4 and reverse T3. Small studies in euthyroid patients reported symptom improvement and normalized body temperature. The American Thyroid Association does not recognize "Wilson's syndrome" and advises against this protocol. Deborah's own approach, adding T3 when free T3 is low despite adequate free T4, is lab-guided and differs from it.
